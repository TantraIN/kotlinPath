<KeyIdea>
A staged rollout limits **how many users a bad release reaches** — but only while you are watching a
metric that would tell you it is bad, and only if halting is a decision someone is allowed to make
quickly.
</KeyIdea>

## The tracks

| Track | Who gets it | Review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal | Up to 100 testers you list | Minutes; effectively none |
| Closed | An opt-in list or a Google Group | Full review |
| Open | Anyone who joins from the listing | Full review |
| Production | Everyone | Full review |

Internal testing is the one to use constantly, because it is available within minutes and needs no
review. Closed testing is where a real beta group lives. Open testing is public and appears on your
listing, which is a marketing decision as much as a testing one.

Two things about review. It usually takes hours to a day, but a first submission or an app that touches
a sensitive permission can take a week — so the release calendar has to allow for it. And review happens
per track, so promoting a build that already passed review on one track is fast.

**Pre-launch reports** run your build on real devices in Google's lab and report crashes, accessibility
problems and performance issues before anyone installs it. They are free and most teams ignore them.

## Staged rollout

```text title="A typical ramp" numbered
day 0   1%     watch crash-free rate and ANR rate
day 1   5%     if stable
day 2   20%
day 4   50%
day 7   100%
```

The percentages matter less than the pauses. A rollout that goes 1% to 100% in an hour is not staged;
it is a delayed full release.

What to watch is a short list: crash-free users, ANR rate, and one behavioural metric that would catch a
functional break — sign-ins per hour, orders per hour, whatever your app is for. Crash-free rate alone
misses the release where nothing crashes and the checkout button does nothing.

## Halting and the one-way door

```text title="What you can and cannot do" numbered
halt a rollout          -> yes, immediately; new users stop receiving it
resume a rollout        -> yes
roll back a rollout     -> NO
```

There is no un-ship. Halting stops the release reaching more users, but everyone who already updated
keeps the broken version — Android has no downgrade path for an installed app. The only forward fix is
a new build with a higher `versionCode`, which needs review.

Two consequences follow. First, the ramp should be slow enough that a halt limits the damage
meaningfully; halting at 50% saves half your users, which is much less impressive than it sounds.
Second, anything that might need turning off should be behind a Remote Config flag from the quality
phase — that is the only mechanism that reaches users who already updated, and it works in minutes
rather than days.

<Detail summary="Managed publishing, and the release calendar">
**Managed publishing** decouples review from release: builds go through review and then sit approved
until you press publish. Without it, an approved build goes live whenever review happens to finish,
which might be 2am on a Saturday.

That matters because of when things go wrong. A release that goes out on a Friday afternoon has the
weekend to accumulate one-star reviews before anyone reads the metrics, and the one-star reviews
outlive the bug — Play's rating is cumulative, so a bad weekend depresses the score for months.

The practical shape most teams converge on:

- Ship Monday to Wednesday, in the morning, with someone watching.
- Internal track continuously; production on a fixed cadence.
- A named person who can halt without a meeting.
- The rollback plan written down, which is the Remote Config flag, not a new build.

None of that is technical, and all of it decides how much a staged rollout is actually worth.
</Detail>

## Common mistakes

<Pitfall title="Five, and the second makes staging pointless">
**Watching only crash-free rate.** A broken flow that does not crash is invisible to it.

**Staging without watching.** The same outcome as shipping to 100%, with extra steps.

**Expecting to roll back.** You cannot; users who updated keep the broken build.

**Friday releases.** The weekend fills with one-star reviews that outlive the fix.

**Ignoring pre-launch reports.** Free crash and accessibility findings on real devices.
</Pitfall>

## Practice

1. Ship to internal testing and note how long it takes to become available.
2. Start a 1% rollout, then halt it, and confirm what happens to users who already updated.
3. Add one behavioural metric to your release dashboard alongside crash-free rate.
4. Read a pre-launch report for your current build.
5. Put your riskiest new feature behind a Remote Config flag before the next release.

## Recap

| Thing | One line |
| --- | --- |
| Internal | Up to 100 testers, minutes, no review |
| Closed | A real beta group; full review |
| Open | Public and on your listing |
| Review | Hours to a week; plan the calendar around it |
| Pre-launch report | Free findings on real devices |
| Staged rollout | Limits reach, only if you watch and can halt |
| Watch | Crash-free, ANR, and one behavioural metric |
| Halt | Stops new users; does not undo the update |
| No rollback | The fix is a new build, or a Remote Config flag |
| Managed publishing | Decides when a release goes live, not review |

<Interview title="A question about what a rollout actually protects you from">
"How would you release a risky change?"

Behind a Remote Config flag first, defaulting to off, so the code ships inert and I can enable it
without a release — and disable it in minutes if it goes wrong. That matters because a staged rollout
cannot be rolled back: halting stops new users receiving the build, but everyone who already updated
keeps it, and Android has no downgrade.

Then a slow ramp — 1%, 5%, 20% with real pauses — and someone actually watching. The watchlist has to
include a behavioural metric, not just crash-free rate and ANRs, because the failure a staged rollout is
meant to catch is often one where nothing crashes and a button silently does nothing. And I would ship
early in the week, in the morning, because a Friday release accumulates one-star reviews over a weekend
and Play's rating is cumulative — the bug gets fixed and the score does not recover for months.
</Interview>
